# Findings

Generated by `benchmarks/run_all.py`. Corpus: 4,000 synthetic identities,
37,700 labelled pairs, config fingerprint recorded in
`data/generated/snapshot-v0.1.0/manifest.json`.

Primary metric throughout: **recall at a 1% false-positive budget**. Alert
capacity is fixed, so the operational question is never "what is the F1" but
"how many true matches do we catch inside the budget we have".

**The aggregate is a weak signal and should not be the citation.** The
0.2-point lead of `indic_phonetic_dravidian` over Soundex is *statistically*
real across 15 seeds (+0.0017 [+0.0005, +0.0028]) but operationally meaningless.
Plain `indic_phonetic` is genuinely **worse** than Soundex on the mixed set
(−0.018, 0/15 seeds favouring it). The corpus averages seven families with very
different profiles and the mean hides all of it — the per-family table below is
the result worth quoting. Every comparison here is tested in §11.

**A separate finding hides in the R@0.1% column.** Every unmodified phonetic
method scores exactly zero at a 0.1% budget. Not because the phonology fails —
because their scores are too coarse to place a threshold there. Soundex emits 74
distinct values across 31,500 pairs; if none of those 74 operating points sits
under the budget, there is nothing to tune. `token_sort_levenshtein`, with
continuous scores and worse phonetics, still recovers 0.183.

**This turns out to be fixable, and §7 measures the fix.** Blending a cheap
continuous tie-breaker into the coarse score recovers 0.148–0.161 in that region
at a cost of under 0.006 recall at the 1% budget, with AUC-PR *rising*. The two
tie-broken rows above are the strongest all-round methods measured.

### 2.1 The control condition validates the claim

Levenshtein recovers **0.874** on `noise` — OCR damage, keystroke slips,
separator inconsistency, case, diacritics. It is a competent fuzzy matcher.

The same matcher scores **0.026** on `affix` and **0.179** on `structure`.

That contrast is the whole argument. If edit distance degraded uniformly, the
story would be "fuzzy matching is hard" and there would be nothing to publish.
It degrades *selectively*, on exactly the families that encode Indic
orthographic and structural convention, while handling generic data corruption
well. The noise family exists to license that inference and it does.

### 2.2 Affixes are the cheapest available win

Prepending "Shri", adding or dropping Singh/Kaur/Devi, appending `S/o Vinod
Sharma` — these drop Levenshtein to **0.026** and Jaro-Winkler to **0.088**.

Every phonetic matcher here holds at ~0.44, and the reason is not phonetics: it
is that they strip honorifics and truncate at relational qualifiers before
comparing. That is a preprocessing step, not an algorithm change, and it is
available to anyone running screening today for the cost of a token list.

### 2.3 The Indic encoder wins on transliteration, and only there

On `transliteration` the voicing-merged encoder reaches **0.869** against
Soundex 0.783 and Jaro-Winkler 0.488. Across 15 corpus seeds that lead is
**+0.092 [+0.085, +0.093], 15/15 seeds** — solid (§11).

**The strict-mode encoder does not beat Soundex here, and an earlier version of
this report said it did.** At 0.789 vs 0.783 the gap is +0.004 with a 95%
interval of [−0.006, +0.015] and only 5 of 15 seeds favouring it. That is no
difference, and the claim is withdrawn. What genuinely wins on this family is
the **voicing merge specifically** (+0.087 [+0.081, +0.094], 15/15), consistent
with Dravidian scripts not marking voicing on stops — not the Indic encoder in
general.

On **Bengali anglicisation it is the worst method tested (0.117)** — below
Levenshtein's 0.360 and Soundex's 0.394. Chatterjee/Chattopadhyay is a
historical divergence, not a phonological one; the two forms share four leading
characters out of thirteen and no phonology recovers that. Soundex does better
here essentially by accident: its aggressive truncation to four characters
happens to preserve the shared prefix.

On `arabic_persian` Soundex (0.882) beats the Indic encoder (0.760). The
family's variance is dominated by lexical alternation — Mohammed/Mahomed,
Abdurrahman/Abdul Rahman — which is enumerated, not derived, and again not
phonological.

**The honest summary: the encoder solves the problem it models and nothing
else.** That is a narrower claim than "an Indic-adapted encoder is better", and
it is the one the data supports.

### 2.4 Cross-script is a floor of zero

Every Latin-script method scores 0.000. This is definitional — the strings share
no characters — and it isolates the one capability only a multilingual encoder
can supply. MuRIL and LaBSE would be the interesting comparison and **were not
run here**; the environment had no access to model weights. Their absence from
this table is missing data, not a zero.

## 3. Degradation with severity

Recall at 1% FPR against number of stacked transformations.

| Matcher | s1 | s2 | s3 | s4 | s5 | s1→s5 retained |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| soundex | 0.640 | 0.388 | 0.318 | 0.200 | 0.162 | 25% |
| indic_phonetic_dravidian | 0.640 | 0.386 | 0.332 | 0.198 | 0.172 | **27%** |
| indic_phonetic | 0.618 | 0.372 | 0.302 | 0.184 | 0.146 | 24% |
| double_metaphone | 0.584 | 0.304 | 0.276 | 0.138 | 0.108 | 18% |
| token_sort_levenshtein | 0.598 | 0.284 | 0.178 | 0.068 | 0.054 | 9% |
| levenshtein | 0.544 | 0.204 | 0.128 | 0.044 | 0.036 | **7%** |
| jaro_winkler | 0.494 | 0.164 | 0.104 | 0.040 | 0.022 | 4% |

Every method degrades steeply. The interesting quantity is the *shape*: string
metrics lose 93% of their recall between severity 1 and 5, phonetic methods lose
~75%.

The steepness is partly a property of the construction — severity 5 stacks five
rules from distinct families, which is more damage than most real records carry.
Severity 1–2 is the realistic operating range for a single well-maintained
source; severity 3+ models records that have passed through several systems.

Severity is an ordinal count of rule applications, not a distance. Mean
normalised edit distance from seed rises 0.24 → 0.60 across the range, with
substantial within-level spread; see METHODOLOGY.md §2.3.

## 4. Hard negatives

Recall at 1% FPR against each negative construction separately.

| Matcher | easy | same_given_ diff_surname | same_surname_ diff_given | shared_ initials | suffix_only |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| levenshtein | 0.746 | 0.179 | 0.152 | 0.169 | 0.259 |
| jaro_winkler | 0.696 | 0.144 | 0.214 | 0.242 | 0.164 |
| soundex | 0.817 | 0.440 | 0.440 | **0.000** | 0.355 |
| double_metaphone | 0.855 | 0.361 | 0.361 | **0.000** | 0.290 |
| char_embedding_svd | **0.962** | 0.347 | 0.291 | 0.128 | 0.383 |
| indic_phonetic | 0.874 | **0.550** | **0.581** | **0.000** | 0.338 |
| indic_phonetic_dravidian | 0.882 | 0.540 | **0.601** | **0.000** | 0.357 |

**Token weighting is where the Indic matcher earns its keep.** On
`same_surname_diff_given` it reaches 0.601 against Soundex's 0.440 and
Levenshtein's 0.152 — a 4× improvement over edit distance. The mechanism is
weighting Kumar/Devi/Singh at a quarter, so two records agreeing only on filler
do not score as a match. This is orthogonal to phonetics and could be bolted
onto any matcher.

**Every phonetic matcher scores 0.000 on `shared_initials`.** "R. Venkatesh" vs
"Rajagopal Venkatesh" — the phonetic matchers all award partial credit for a
matching initial phoneme, which puts these negatives above the 1% threshold. The
continuous-score matchers do better (Jaro-Winkler 0.242) simply by scoring them
lower. This is a genuine weakness of initial-tolerant matching and the direct
cost of the 0.55 partial-credit rule in METHODOLOGY.md §1.4.

**`identical_collision` scores 0.000 for everything, by construction.** Both
sides are the same string. It is excluded from all headline numbers and reported
here only to fix the ceiling: at a realistic rate of same-name collisions, **no
name-only method can exceed it**. This is why screening needs date of birth and
address, and it is not a property of any algorithm.

## 5. Alert volume

Alerts per 100,000 names screened at a 1% FPR operating point, converted to
analyst time at a stated **4 minutes per alert**.

At a base rate of 1 true match per 10,000 names, a 1% false-positive rate
produces roughly **1,000 false alerts per 100,000 names** — about **67 analyst
hours** — to surface around 0.04 true matches. Precision at that operating point
is under 0.005 for every method tested.

Two things follow.

**The headline recall differences barely move the cost.** Every method here sits
at the same order of magnitude of alert volume, because volume at a 1% budget is
dominated by the FPR term, which is fixed by the budget. Choosing between
matchers at a fixed FPR budget is a *recall* decision, not a cost decision.

**Cost separates by cost class, not by accuracy.** At 100,000 names against a
5,000-entry watchlist, the pairwise comparison count is 5×10⁸. At 7.8 µs/pair
Soundex takes ~65 CPU-minutes; at 14.5 µs the Indic encoder takes ~2 CPU-hours;
an LLM reranker at ~1 s/pair would take **16 CPU-years** and is only viable
behind a cascade that reduces the candidate set by four or more orders of
magnitude.

The cascade measured here (`ngram_jaccard` blocking at 0.25 → `indic_phonetic`)
reaches 0.335 R@1% FPR at 15.0 µs/pair. Its blocking recall ceiling and rerank
fraction are recorded in `results.json`; the ceiling is the number that matters,
because no reranker recovers a match the blocker discarded.

**These volumes are upper bounds.** Hard negatives are drawn from the confusable
region rather than uniformly, so measured FPR exceeds what a uniform draw at the
same base rate would give. Relative comparisons between matchers are unaffected —
all share the negative set.

## 6. Fairness

### 6.1 The hypothesis was not confirmed

The pre-registered hypothesis: **name families with higher variant density
generate systematically higher false-positive rates**, so members of some
communities are disproportionately flagged through a purely technical mechanism.
Arabic/Persian names — the highest variant-density family in the corpus — were
the expected worst case.

**The data does not support this.** At a single global threshold set from pooled
data at a 1% budget:

Soundex, global threshold 0.90, pooled FPR 0.0027. Disparity is the ratio to
the pooled rate, with a 95% interval built from Wilson bounds.

| Origin | FPR | 95% CI | Disparity | Disparity CI | n (negatives) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **telugu** | **0.0169** | [0.0111, 0.0257] | **6.27×** | **[2.83, 13.86]** | 1,241 |
| hindi_belt | 0.0021 | [0.0008, 0.0055] | 0.79× | [0.21, 2.95] | 1,872 |
| gujarati | 0.0020 | [0.0003, 0.0111] | 0.73× | [0.09, 5.98] | 506 |
| **arabic_persian** | **0.0010** | [0.0002, 0.0058] | **0.39×** | [0.05, 3.15] | 966 |
| bengali | 0.0000 | [0.0000, 0.0050] | 0.00× | [0.00, 2.68] | 769 |
| kannada | 0.0000 | [0.0000, 0.0062] | 0.00× | [0.00, 2.68] | 617 |

Arabic/Persian sits near the **bottom** at 0.39× the pooled rate — and its
interval spans 1, so it is not distinguishable from the pooled rate at all. The
highest belongs to **Telugu** at 6.27×, whose interval excludes 1 by a wide
margin. The pattern holds across matchers (Levenshtein: Telugu 0.0274, highest;
Jaro-Winkler: Punjabi 0.0224, highest).

So the hypothesised effect is absent, and the opposite ordering is present with
one stratum clearly separated.

### 6.2 The disparity is not an artefact of the frequency tiers

§6.3 of the first version of this report said the pattern was "substantially an
artefact of corpus construction" and that artefact could not be separated from
finding. That was an honest caveat and a useless one — a reader could not tell
whether the result was robust or manufactured. So it was tested.

`benchmarks/tier_sensitivity.py` re-runs corpus generation and the whole
fairness analysis under three tier regimes: as declared; with tier labels
**permuted within each origin** (preserving each origin's tier histogram exactly
while randomising which names are common); and with **every tier flattened to 1**,
removing frequency structure entirely.

| Origin | Declared | Shuffled mean | Shuffled [min, max] | Highest in | Uniform tiers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| telugu | 0.0154 | 0.0124 | [0.0000, 0.0351] | **7 / 10** | 0.0122 |
| kannada | 0.0000 | 0.0008 | [0.0000, 0.0045] | 1 / 10 | 0.0046 |
| bengali | 0.0000 | 0.0018 | [0.0000, 0.0085] | 1 / 10 | 0.0044 |
| hindi_belt | 0.0000 | 0.0013 | [0.0000, 0.0037] | 1 / 10 | 0.0019 |
| arabic_persian | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | [0.0000, 0.0000] | 0 / 10 | 0.0000 |
| tamil | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | [0.0000, 0.0000] | 0 / 10 | 0.0000 |

**The disparity survives all three regimes.** Telugu is highest in 7 of 10
shuffled trials, its shuffled mean (0.0124) sits close to its declared value
(0.0154), and under completely uniform tiers it is *still* highest at 0.0122
while every other stratum stays at or near zero.

So the finding is **not** driven by the tier judgements. Scrambling them, and
even deleting them, leaves it standing. That is a materially stronger position
than §6.1 could claim on its own.

### 6.3 But the mechanism proposed in the first version was wrong

The original explanation was surname concentration: Telugu drawing from a small
set of very high-frequency surnames (Reddy, Rao, Naidu) producing dense
collision neighbourhoods.

**That does not hold up.** Correlating each origin's surname-inventory size
against its false-positive rate under uniform tiers gives **r = −0.03** —
nothing. Telugu has 30 distinct surnames, mid-range; Tamil has 21, the fewest,
and a false-positive rate of zero. Inventory size does not explain it.

What does correlate is **mean token count per name: r = +0.69**.

| Origin | Mean tokens | FPR (uniform tiers) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| telugu | 2.78 | 0.0122 |
| punjabi | 2.59 | 0.0000 |
| hindi_belt | 2.50 | 0.0019 |
| kannada | 2.43 | 0.0046 |
| bengali | 2.33 | 0.0044 |
| tamil | 2.14 | 0.0000 |

The proposed mechanism is that **more distinguishing tokens per name means a
hard negative shares more matching tokens**, pushing its similarity above a
globally-set threshold. Telugu's dominant template is patronymic + given +
surname; Tamil's is a two-token initial-plus-name. A negative pair differing in
one token out of three scores higher than one differing in one out of two.

**Punjabi is the informative exception** and supports the mechanism rather than
contradicting it: 2.59 mean tokens but zero false positives, because its third
token is usually Singh or Kaur, which the matcher weights at 0.25. Tokens only
inflate similarity when they carry identifying information — which is precisely
what the token-weighting in §4 was built to exploit.

### 6.4 What can and cannot be said

**Can be said.** At a single global threshold, false-positive rates differ by
roughly an order of magnitude across name-origin categories, and this survives
scrambling and removing the frequency tiers. A globally-tuned threshold is
therefore mis-calibrated for some name structures — a robust property of one
threshold over heterogeneous name-length distributions.

**Cannot be said.** That any real community is disproportionately flagged in
production screening. This is synthetic data with hand-built templates, and
per-origin mean token count is a property of the templates I wrote.

**The hypothesis this project set out to test is not confirmed.** Variant
density does not drive false-positive rate; Arabic/Persian, the highest
variant-density family, sits at zero in every regime tested. Variant density
inflates the distance between spellings of *one* name, which costs recall, not
precision against *other* people.

Remaining caveats: n = 11 origins, so r = +0.69 rests on eleven points and is
suggestive rather than established. The token-count mechanism was generated by
this analysis and is not independently tested. 20% of identities are
`origin_ambiguous`. Several strata sit at exactly zero, so ratios against them
are undefined and the pooled reference is used instead.

## 7. Score granularity: the zeros at a tight budget are fixable

§1 noted that every phonetic method scores exactly 0.000 at a 0.1% FPR budget.
That was an observation, not a diagnosis. This experiment tests whether the cause
is phonology or representation.

A matcher emitting *N* distinct scores has *N* available operating points. If
none sits under the budget, its recall there is zero regardless of matching
quality. Blending a small amount of a continuous signal (Jaro-Winkler at weight
0.15) breaks ties without relitigating any phonetic decision.

| Matcher | Distinct scores | R@0.1% FPR | R@1% FPR | AUC-PR | µs/pair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| soundex | 74 | **0.000** | 0.355 | 0.838 | 5.4 |
| soundex + tiebreak | 15,250 | **0.148** | 0.349 | 0.851 | 9.0 |
| indic_phonetic | 531 | **0.000** | 0.338 | 0.849 | 8.1 |
| indic_phonetic + tiebreak | 17,118 | **0.161** | 0.333 | 0.852 | 11.5 |

**The hypothesis holds.** Soundex emits 74 distinct scores across 31,500 pairs —
74 operating points, none of them under 0.1% FPR. With a tie-breaker it emits
15,250, and recovers **0.148** recall in a region where it previously had none.

The cost is negligible and the phonology is untouched:

- R@1% FPR moves by −0.006 and −0.005. Across 15 seeds that cost is real rather
  than noise (−0.004 [−0.005, −0.003] for the Indic encoder), but it is four
  tenths of a point against a 13-point gain at the tight budget. The blend does
  not reorder pairs the base matcher already separated.
- **AUC-PR rises** in both cases (0.838→0.851, 0.849→0.852), so this is not a
  precision-for-recall trade.
- Latency roughly doubles in relative terms but stays in single-digit
  microseconds, well inside the cheap cost class.

`indic_phonetic+tiebreak` becomes the strongest all-round method measured: 0.161
at a 0.1% budget, 0.333 at 1%, AUC-PR 0.852, 11.5 µs/pair. It beats
`token_sort_levenshtein` (0.183 at 0.1%) on every other axis while costing a
sixth as much.

**Operational reading.** Any deployed phonetic screening system reporting zero
recall at a tight alert budget is probably not phonology-limited — it is
threshold-limited, and one extra cheap comparison per pair recovers a meaningful
share. No retuning, no new algorithm, no change to which pairs the phonology
treats as equivalent.

## 8. What an alias table is worth

The Indic encoder is the *worst* method tested on Bengali anglicisation (0.117).
Chatterjee/Chattopadhyay is historical, not phonological, so only a lookup table
can reach it — which is what real screening systems maintain as alias lists.

**Read the caveat before the table.** `alias_oracle` uses the same equivalence
tables the corpus was *generated* from. It is an upper bound on what a complete
alias list achieves, not a generalisation result, and must not be cited as
matcher performance. `alias_half` holds out 50% of the classes (45 of 109
classes, 148 of 362 forms) and is the deployable comparison.

| Matcher | Bengali anglicisation | Arabic/Persian | Transliteration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| indic_phonetic (baseline) | 0.117 | 0.760 | 0.789 |
| indic_phonetic + alias_half | 0.548 | 0.846 | 0.788 |
| indic_phonetic + alias_oracle *(upper bound)* | 0.958 | 0.944 | 0.788 |
| soundex (reference) | 0.394 | 0.882 | 0.783 |
| alias_exact (table only, no phonetics) | 0.958 | 0.944 | **0.021** |

### 8.1 Alias coverage buys recall linearly, and nothing generalises

41% class coverage moves Bengali recall from 0.117 to 0.548 — about 51% of the
way to the oracle's 0.958. That is close enough to linear that the conclusion is
unambiguous: **an alias table helps precisely and only for the names already
enumerated in it.** There is no transfer to unlisted variants, because there is
no rule to transfer — that is what "lexical" means.

For a compliance function this is the actionable form of the finding. Alias-list
investment has a predictable, measurable return proportional to coverage of the
name families you actually screen, and no return beyond it. It is not a
technology decision; it is a data-curation budget.

### 8.2 Phonetics and alias tables are complementary, not substitutes

The bottom row is the important one. `alias_exact` — table lookup with no fuzzy
matching at all — reaches **0.958** on Bengali anglicisation and **0.021** on
transliteration. The Indic encoder does the reverse: 0.789 on transliteration,
0.117 on Bengali.

Neither mechanism substitutes for the other, and the two failure modes are
disjoint. A system with only phonetics misses every historical anglicisation; a
system with only an alias list misses every spelling it has not enumerated. The
combination (`alias_half`) is the only configuration competitive on both.

Alias normalisation also lifts Arabic/Persian from 0.760 to 0.846, for the same
reason: that family's variance is substantially lexical (Mohammed/Mahomed) rather
than phonological. It leaves transliteration untouched (0.789 → 0.788), which is
the correct null result — the phonology already handles that family, and the
table has nothing to add.

## 11. Are these differences real?

Every number above came from one corpus draw. §11 tests whether the gaps
survive resampling. `benchmarks/significance.py`, 15 corpus seeds, 2,500
identities each.

**Method.** Paired per-seed differences. All matchers score the *same* corpus in
each replicate, so their errors are strongly positively correlated — a seed
producing hard variants lowers everyone together. Putting a CI on each matcher
and checking for overlap would absorb that shared variance into both intervals
and is conservative to the point of uselessness. The delta cancels it. A sign
test cross-checks distribution-free.

| Claim | Delta | 95% CI | Seeds | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Indic-dravidian > Soundex, transliteration | +0.092 | [+0.085, +0.099] | 15/15 | **real** |
| Voicing merge helps, transliteration | +0.087 | [+0.081, +0.094] | 15/15 | **real** |
| Indic > Jaro-Winkler, transliteration | +0.318 | [+0.294, +0.342] | 15/15 | **real** |
| Indic > Levenshtein, transliteration | +0.214 | [+0.183, +0.244] | 15/15 | **real** |
| Tie-break recovers 0.1% budget (Soundex) | +0.146 | [+0.136, +0.156] | 15/15 | **real** |
| Tie-break recovers 0.1% budget (Indic) | +0.129 | [+0.105, +0.152] | 14/15 | **real** |
| Indic-dravidian > Soundex, headline | +0.002 | [+0.001, +0.003] | 13/15 | real but negligible |
| Tie-break cost at 1% budget | −0.004 | [−0.005, −0.003] | 1/15 | real, small |
| **Indic > Soundex, transliteration** | **+0.004** | **[−0.006, +0.015]** | **5/15** | **NO DIFFERENCE** |
| Indic > Soundex, headline | −0.018 | [−0.020, −0.017] | 0/15 | real, **reversed** |

### 11.1 One claim withdrawn

The bottom two rows were overclaimed in the first version of this report. The
strict-mode Indic encoder does **not** beat Soundex on transliteration — the
interval spans zero and fewer than half the seeds favour it — and on the mixed
headline set Soundex is reliably *better*.

The defensible version of the contribution is narrower: **the voicing merge is
what wins**, by about nine points on transliteration, reproducibly across every
seed tested. The encoder's other design choices are not shown to beat Soundex.

### 11.2 What this does and does not cover

Covers variance from **corpus sampling only** — which identities get assembled
and which transformations fire.

Does **not** cover uncertainty in the component inventory, the hand-assigned
frequency tiers, or the rule set. Those are systematic, and no resampling
procedure can see them: every replicate draws from the same inventory I wrote.
`tier_sensitivity.py` addresses the tier component separately; the rest is in
§10.

So "real" here means "not explained by which names happened to be drawn". It
does not mean "would replicate on real records".

---

## 10. Limitations

Beyond the fairness caveats in §6.3 and the alias-oracle caveat in §8:

1. **Frequency tiers are not measured.** Highest-value fix available.
2. **Synthetic variants may not match real distributions.** Rules are
   phonotactically constrained but not lexically filtered, so some forms are
   plausible-but-unattested (Ganguly → Ghanguly is the same rule as the attested
   Gosh → Ghosh).
3. **Positives are canonical-vs-variant**, matching the screening shape of a
   clean list entry against a messy record. Variant-vs-variant is strictly
   harder and is not measured.
4. **Cross-script coverage is partial** — curated pairs only, ~50–100 tokens per
   script.
5. **The character-embedding matcher saw the corpus vocabulary** during its
   unsupervised fit. No pair labels, but treat its numbers as a mild upper bound.
6. **The 4-minute alert-handling assumption** is stated, not measured. Every
   analyst-hour figure scales linearly with it.
7. **Severity is a construction parameter**, not a calibrated difficulty scale.
   Comparisons across severity levels are ordinal only.
